# Historical notes

## Scope of the chapter

Jejaka places a fictional journalist in a reconstructed part of Surabaya during November 1945. Raka Pradana, Sari, Damar, their dialogue, the choices, the injury, and the precise sequence of attacks are authored fiction. Their names do not identify people found in the cited archives. The story does not claim to reproduce a recovered notebook.

The chapter uses a historical city and a historical conflict. It does not establish an exact day, local hour, unit position, weapon, target, casualty, weather condition, evacuation route, or building-specific damage state. The narrative compresses time for an authored encounter. Its brief quieter opening does not mean the city had been peaceful before the visitor arrived.

## What the evidence supports

The National Army Museum’s account identifies 10 November 1945 as the beginning of the major offensive and describes a broader campaign through the month. It represents a modern British institutional interpretation and does not supply a complete Indonesian civilian account (National Army Museum, n.d.).

The UTS Library collection record identifies P. R. S. Mani’s wartime dispatches and diary entries covering September to November 1945. These provide a situated contemporary perspective and context for reporting during the conflict. Raka’s dialogue and diary are not quotations from Mani (Mani & Goodall, 2014).

The municipal museum record concerning the 9 November ultimatum and the National Army Museum’s discussion of an 8 November communication should remain distinct. Their references need not describe one identical act of issue, delivery, or public dissemination. Neither date establishes the hour or place of Raka’s fictional episode.

The Hotel Yamato flag incident in September and Mallaby’s death in late October precede the chapter’s November setting. The experience does not reenact either event, identify Mallaby’s killer, or place those episodes into its short route.

## The reconstructed district

The environmental base uses a wartime map revised from March 1943 aerial photographs, together with architectural photographs of different dates. It supplies approximate relationships among the river, crossing, streets, and selected landmarks. Many module widths, rear elevations, heights, facade colours, shopfronts, and background parcels are authored reconstruction choices.

The route’s positions are local production coordinates rather than survey or GPS coordinates. Its western approach was moved to avoid conflicts with the exported landmark meshes; that movement does not verify a new historical street alignment. A modern surviving building, restored facade, or visiting pin does not prove its exact form or condition in November 1945. Fire, smoke, debris, displaced belongings, and characters belong to the fictional presentation layer. They must not silently change the evidence status of the historical base.

The visitor’s final view across the river shows a dramatized situation. It does not establish that a specific documented landmark burned at that moment. The camera’s inability to see an attacker is a narrative restriction, not evidence that no attacker existed.

## Reading the notebook

The notebook distinguishes three kinds of entry:

1. **Observation:** Raka describes something visible or audible from his position.
2. **Attributed account:** Raka names the fictional speaker and records what that person says.
3. **Uncertainty:** Raka states what he cannot establish.

Nine story beats can add notebook entries. The final resting view and curatorial afterword do not add entries after Raka passes on the book. The choices alter which fictional observations survive. They do not change historical outcomes, validate counterfactual military claims, or supply evidence about real residents. A choice to assist or inspect the road is a local dramatic action; it is not a test of the visitor’s moral worth.

## Representation

The conflict involved Indonesian independence fighters and civilians, British forces, and Indian troops serving within British command structures. Their identities should not collapse into a generic opposing army. The first chapter keeps attackers outside the visible scene rather than introduce unverified uniforms or tactical positions.

Sari’s and Damar’s ordinary concerns prevent the district from becoming an empty battlefield. Their invented circumstances do not stand for the experiences of all residents. The story leaves Sari’s later fate and Damar’s brother’s whereabouts unknown.

## Source access and rights

The UTS collection page and the National Army Museum page were opened during preparation of this chapter. The municipal exhibit page was available in the project’s prior research record; a new retrieval timed out. No new claim depends on that unsuccessful retrieval. The wartime map and architectural evidence follow the supplied reconstruction’s source register and attribution records.

The package links to the archival and museum sources. It does not redistribute their full documents, film, or photographs under an assumed open licence. Production imagery and sound are not archival evidence. Asset-specific licences remain separate from historical source status.
